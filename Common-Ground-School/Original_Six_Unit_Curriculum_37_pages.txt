PROGRAM AT A GLANCE
This is an investigative school, not a confessional enrollment program. Learners
compare observable conditions, historical evidence, ancient narrative witnesses,
interpretations, authority claims, and consequences. The teacher is responsible for
accurate presentation and safe discussion, not for producing a predetermined
religious answer.

The educational sequence is intentionally different from the founder’s own Christ-
centered theological account of StillPoint. That account may be introduced as one
interpretation or a voluntary advanced reading. It cannot function as a required
answer or as proof of learning.

The shared inquiry
What can we establish about reality, history, and the words of our sources before
deciding what those sources require us to believe? When institutions organize a
reality they did not create, what distinguishes responsible coordination from
unjustified possession?

What participants produce
Every session ends with an individual ledger entry: at least one observable
statement, two accurately located text observations, one documented difference, a
labeled interpretation, an authority question, and a possible human consequence.
Learners may adopt, reject, or suspend a theological conclusion.

Preparation and accessibility
Provide short source excerpts with names of translations, source locations, and
sufficient context; offer print and screen-readable versions. Do not require any
participant to perform worship, prayer, a devotional reading, or disclosure of
personal belief. Allow a written response instead of speaking on sensitive subjects
(enslavement, killing, disasters, abuse, and death).

The seven rules of the Charter
1. Reality precedes our descriptions of it.
2. Shared material is presented before differences, but differences are never
     erased.
3. Textual presence does not automatically establish historical occurrence.
4. Interpretation is identified as interpretation, including the facilitator’s.
5. Authority claims are examined; position or status never substitutes for evidence.
6. The learner retains responsibility for their own conclusion.
7. Reciprocity is absolute: the school, StillPoint, and the facilitator face the same
     scrutiny.

UNIT 1 | Reality, the Day, and the Beginning of Rest
Creation, observable time, Sabbath, release, and human responsibility

Duration: 75 minutes • Sources are witnesses to be examined, not mandatory beliefs.


Purpose
Before asking which religion is right, begin with a day. The student should be able
to distinguish Earth’s rotation, a community’s choice of date boundary, and the
religious meaning given to that boundary. From this foundation the class examines
creation narratives and the extension of rest to persons under others’ authority.

Primary reading set
Genesis 1:1–2:3; Exodus 20:8–11; Deuteronomy 5:12–15; Mark 2:27; Leviticus 25:1–
13; Deuteronomy 15:1–11; Qur’an 7:54, 50:38, 2:65, 16:124; Jubilees 2 and 6; 1 Enoch
72–82 (selected).

Learning outcomes
    Distinguish a physical day from a convention for counting a day.
    Identify the six-day creation structure without imposing an identical seventh-
     day claim on all witnesses.
    Compare creation-grounded rest with liberation-grounded rest.
    Distinguish weekly cessation, seventh-year agricultural release, debt release,
     and Jubilee.
    Test a modern proposal for protected time without treating its mathematics as
     scriptural command.

Part 1 — Reality before our descriptions (10 minutes)
Open with a question, not a sermon: “What would happen in the sky if nobody
knew what Tuesday meant?” Invite observations about rotation, darkness, light,
and local sunrise and sunset.

Write two columns: observable cycle and social convention. Rotation and light
variation belong in the first column. Choosing midnight, sunrise, or sunset as the
start of a numbered date belongs in the second. Do not treat the social convention
as fictitious: agreed dates have real consequences.

Have two learners explain one sunset-to-sunset cycle without assuming that sunset
is the only possible legal boundary. At polar latitudes seasonal sunrise and sunset
may not occur daily; the rotation remains measurable.

Ask: “Who benefits when all parties agree which date it is? Who can make that
agreement binding?”

Part 2 — Creation narratives, shared before different (20 minutes)
Read Genesis 1:1–2:3, Qur’an 7:54 and 50:38, and selected Jubilees 2. Learners
underline descriptions of six-day creation in each witness. Keep the common
finding narrow: these texts present God as creator across six days or periods.

Next mark differences. Genesis explicitly blesses the seventh day and says God
ceased creating; Jubilees connects seventh-day sanctity with covenantal
observance; Qur’an 50:38 says creation did not make God weary. It does not
reproduce the Genesis divine-cessation account in that verse.

Do not presume the texts provide a scientific chronology or that “day” must carry
the same interpretive meaning in every tradition.

Working question: “If texts describe the same broad pattern but make different
claims about the seventh day, what exactly can the school affirm they share?”

Part 3 — Rest and who receives it (15 minutes)
Compare Exodus 20:8–11 and Deuteronomy 5:12–15. Ask groups to list all the
people and animals to whom the command applies. Include household authorities,
children, enslaved servants, resident outsiders, and livestock. Note the texts’
particular ancient social world without pretending it matches modern employment.

Have learners mark the grounds given: creation in Exodus, slavery and liberation
in Deuteronomy. Add Mark 2:27 as a Christian textual witness on the Sabbath’s
purpose. Ask students to explain that saying without requiring them to agree with
Christian theology.

Ask: “Can the rest of one household member be sustained by transferring
uncompensated labor to somebody with less power? What makes a prohibition of
work socially meaningful?”

Part 4 — Beyond one day (10 minutes)
Read brief excerpts from Leviticus 25 and Deuteronomy 15. Sort weekly Sabbath,
agricultural seventh-year rest, seven-year debt release, and Jubilee land/servitude
provisions as four different rules, not one generic “release.”

Identify the fiftieth-year Jubilee wording and the distinctions the larger chapter
draws among property and forms of servitude. Ask which claims are interrupted
and which persist.

Historical restraint: the existence of a legal command in a source does not by itself
prove how consistently communities practiced it.

Part 5 — Guided Common Ground Ledger (15 minutes)
1. Reality: Earth rotates, and humans require sleep and recovery. 2. History:
calendars vary; Enochic and Jubilees witnesses attest a 364-day ideal. 3. Scripture:
pinpoint six-day and Sabbath clauses. 4. Agreement/Difference: report the shared
framework and distinguish divine cessation from denial of weariness.

5. Interpretation: sacred command, literary pattern, social protection, or StillPoint’s
proposed stop–rest–start rhythm. 6. Authority: who has power to compel labor or
define the calendar? 7. Human consequence: who rests, who must still care for
others, who bears the cost?

Optional numerical exercise, identified as a modern proposal: a seven-day week is
168 hours. One-seventh is 24 hours. One-fifth is 33.6 hours, or 33 hours 36 minutes.
If StillPoint is exactly one-fifth and includes the 24-hour Sabbath, the additional
protected span is 9 hours 36 minutes—not another Sabbath.

Important timing distinction: an exact 33-hour-36-minute protected interval does
not invariably end Sunday at dawn. A sunset-to-sunrise boundary varies with
geography and season. The school presents those as design choices, not
interchangeable numbers.

Part 6 — Closing question (5 minutes)
“When human beings organize a reality they did not create, how do we distinguish
responsible stewardship from unjustified possession?” Each learner offers one
statement grounded in a passage or observation and one question they have not
resolved.

No belief declaration is requested. The session succeeds when participants can
locate textual claims, identify their limits, and keep one another free to disagree.

Independent study
Choose one pair: Genesis 1 and Qur’an 50:38; Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5;
Deuteronomy 15 and Leviticus 25; or Jubilees 6 and 1 Enoch 72–82. Record: (1)
exact claim, (2) question, (3) what further evidence would be needed. Optional:
bring two competing time conventions from ordinary life.

Facilitator restraint
Do not announce a correct cosmology, theological interpretation, or StillPoint civic
policy. Correct source attribution and arithmetic while leaving interpretive
judgments open.

UNIT 2 | The Garden, the Brothers, Human Violence, and
Noah
Provision, boundaries, responsibility, violence, catastrophe, and continuation

Duration: 75 minutes • Sources are witnesses to be examined, not mandatory beliefs.


Purpose
This session investigates how early sacred narratives move from provision into
violated limits, interpersonal violence, widespread corruption, and the Flood. The
class asks not whether every account is historically confirmed, but what each
witness claims and how those claims have been used to justify or resist authority.

Primary reading set
Genesis 2–4 and 6–9; Qur’an 2:35–38, 7:19–25, 5:27–31, 11:25–49, 71:1–28; Jubilees 3–
6; optional 1 Enoch 6–11 as a distinctive pre-Flood witness.

Learning outcomes
    Identify what the Garden, brothers, and Flood narratives genuinely share.
    Separate textual differences: serpent/Satan, named/unnamed brothers,
     mark/crow, and Noah’s sons.
    Distinguish ancient flood traditions from physical confirmation of a universal
     flood.
    Analyze the difference between protective limits and claims of ownership.

Part 1 — Begin with human vulnerability (8 minutes)
Ask: “What does an infant need before it understands any moral rule?” Students list
nourishment, shelter, protection, and relationship. Continue: “What kinds of limits
protect people? When can an imposed limit become abusive?”

Make clear that the observable human capacity for jealousy and killing is not
evidence that a specific Garden or Flood episode happened as narrated. A lived
condition and a text’s explanation belong in different layers of the Ledger.

Part 2 — The Garden: a boundary within provision (15 minutes)
Read Genesis 2:15–17 and 3:1–24 with Qur’an 2:35–38 and 7:19–25; consult Jubilees
3. Ask learners to write four verbs for each story: receive, prohibit, transgress,
respond. Where they differ, keep the difference on the page.

Genesis names a serpent, gives detailed judgments involving labor, pain, and
expulsion, and contains particular statements about access to the tree of life.
Qur’anic passages identify Satan’s role, include repentance, and frame descent and
guidance in their own manner. Do not paste Genesis’s detailed consequences into
the Qur’an.

Facilitator question: “Does the text portray the boundary as a way to protect
relationship, assert authority, test choice, or some combination? Point to the exact
words.”

Invite three interpretations (historical, literary, theological) and label each as
interpretation rather than common fact.

Part 3 — Two brothers: from comparison to killing (12 minutes)
Pair Genesis 4:1–16 with Qur’an 5:27–31. Both feature offerings and one brother
killing the other. Genesis names Cain and Abel and describes a mark; the Qur’an’s
passage refers to Adam’s two sons without naming them and includes a crow
showing how to conceal the body.

Divide the room into “what both say,” “Genesis alone in this pairing,” and “Qur’an
alone in this pairing.” Ask learners to resist importing later traditional names into
the immediate Qur’anic wording.

Facilitator question: “Does a rejected offering grant the rejected person authority
over someone else’s life? How is violence separated from justified grievance?”

Part 4 — Noah: interruption and survival (12 minutes)
Read Genesis 6:5–9:17 in selected segments, Qur’an 11:25–49, and Jubilees 5–6.
Record the ark, flood judgment, survival, and continuation of life. Then ask whose
family is aboard in each telling.

Genesis presents Noah’s household and post-Flood covenant, including living
creatures. Qur’an 11 narrates a son refusing to board and drowning. Jubilees
organizes the story inside its calendrical retelling. These are not trivial variations.

Optional additional witness: 1 Enoch’s Watchers material supplies a different
explanation of pre-Flood corruption; it is not an independent scientific report.

Part 5 — Evidence and scope sorting (8 minutes)
Write on separate slips: “Many ancient flood stories exist,” “a particular named
Noah lived,” “some regions have experienced catastrophic floods,” “a single global
flood has been geologically demonstrated.” Have learners classify each as
literature, historical hypothesis, observation, or unsupported conclusion.

The exercise tests how to say “we found a shared story” without saying “we proved
the narrated event.”

Part 6 — Seven-layer Ledger (15 minutes)
1. Reality: dependence, jealousy, violence, natural disasters. 2. History: related Near
Eastern flood stories; no direct confirmation of the named protagonists. 3.
Scripture: three narratives with exact locations. 4. Agreement/Difference: shared
sequence and source-specific claims.

5. Interpretation: original sin, moral responsibility, human freedom, judgment, or a
StillPoint theory of interruption; none compelled. 6. Authority: who sets boundaries
and who is accountable when a boundary is abused? 7. Human consequence: how
have stories shaped treatment of children, outsiders, and offenders?

Pair-check: learners must find one overbroad assertion in each other’s entries and
replace it with a claim that cites actual text.

Part 7 — Closing (5 minutes)
“If the early narratives repeatedly show limits placed upon human conduct, how
can communities distinguish justified limits from unlimited human control?” A
learner may state no theological conclusion at all.

Independent study
Choose Genesis 2–3 with Qur’an 7:19–25; Genesis 4 with Qur’an 5:27–31; or Genesis
6–9 with Qur’an 11:25–49. Submit a paragraph containing one common event, one
important difference, and one claim the sources cannot independently prove.

Facilitator restraint
Do not teach the flood as archaeologically confirmed; do not describe the Qur’anic
brothers as named within the immediate verse; do not force a single theory of
inherited guilt on every participant.

UNIT 3 | Abraham to Joseph
Promise, agency, unequal power, the test of the son, betrayal, famine, and reunion

Duration: 75 minutes • Sources are witnesses to be examined, not mandatory beliefs.


Purpose
Learners examine how a promise can be narrated across generations without
granting any character possession of another person or of the entire future. The
lesson connects Abraham, Sarah, Hagar, Ishmael, Isaac, Jacob, and Joseph while
distinguishing shared episodes from later confessional interpretations.

Primary reading set
Genesis 12, 16–18, 21–22, 37, 39–45, 50; Qur’an 2:124–132, 11:69–73, 37:100–107,
Surah 12; Jubilees 12–18, 34, 39–45.

Learning outcomes
     Identify promise as a narrative claim, not a verified land or political title.
     Compare Hagar’s position and the different degrees of agency in Abraham’s
      household.
     Distinguish Isaac named in Genesis from the unnamed son in Qur’an 37:100–
      107.
     Trace Joseph’s narrative using a passage-specific parallel between Genesis and
      Surah 12.
     Test how moral responsibility can persist alongside claims of providence.

Part 1 — Reality and family power (7 minutes)
Open: “Who chooses the conditions of their birth? Who has power over a child, an
employee, or an enslaved person?” Do not demand personal disclosure. Have
students distinguish factual dependence from the claim that dependence removes
dignity or agency.

Clarify that our observations about present social power do not prove a particular
patriarchal story. They provide questions with which to read it.

Part 2 — Abraham’s family and the test (18 minutes)
Read Genesis 12:1–3, 16:1–13, 17:15–22, 18:9–15, 21:8–21, 22:1–19; compare Qur’an
11:69–73, 2:124–132, 37:100–107 and Jubilees 16–18.

Start with common features: Abraham’s calling and descendants, unusual birth
announcement, a moment of testing, a son spared. Then mark differences: Genesis
22 names Isaac; the immediate Qur’anic sacrifice account does not name the son.
Later Muslim identification of Ishmael is interpretation/history of reception, not
wording inside Qur’an 37:100–107.

Introduce Hagar as a person subjected to an extreme power imbalance in Genesis
16. Ask students what that narrative says about being seen and heard. Do not
collapse Hagar and Ishmael into an expendable detour or infer that a character’s
mistreatment was morally approved.

Debate in pairs: “If a promise concerns a future generation, who has authority over
the lives of those through whom it unfolds?” Learners must quote at least one
passage in their response.

Part 3 — Joseph: an extended cross-text story (12 minutes)
Assign groups episodes: dreams and brothers (Genesis 37 / Qur’an 12:4–18);
temptation and accusation (Genesis 39 / Qur’an 12:23–35); prison and dreams
(Genesis 40–41 / Qur’an 12:36–49).

Each group finds one shared event and one distinctive detail; groups reconstruct a
chronological narrative without claiming that every detail matches. Joseph’s
Egyptian historical setting is a topic of investigation, not archaeological proof of
Joseph himself.

Ask: “Who controls Joseph’s movement? Who gains power later? Does new
authority erase the earlier wrong?”

Part 4 — Famine, recognition, and reunion (10 minutes)
Read Genesis 42–45 and 50:15–21 alongside Qur’an 12:58–100. The brothers’ need
for food creates contact; the narrative moves through testing, disclosure, care, and
family reunion.

Genesis 50:20 explicitly juxtaposes the brothers’ harmful intention and a good
intention attributed to God. A learner may compare that with the Qur’an’s account
of divine wisdom without claiming they say every same thing.

Ask: “Can a later beneficial outcome make an earlier harmful act morally good?
What responsibility remains?”

Part 5 — Historical claims clinic (8 minutes)
Give four statements: “These texts mention Abraham”; “Abraham is confirmed by
contemporary inscription”; “Asiatics lived in Egypt”; “Joseph certainly served in an
identified Egyptian court.” Learners sort the statements by strength of evidence.

Explain that broad archaeological knowledge about movements into Egypt, famine,
or dynastic courts is not direct independent proof of these individuals.

Part 6 — Seven-layer Ledger (15 minutes)
1. Reality: family dependence, exploitation, food insecurity. 2. History: witnesses
are old and connected; no independent confirmation of the patriarchs. 3. Scripture:
Genesis, Qur’an, Jubilees with locations. 4. Agreement/Difference: keep Isaac
named/unnamed detail and Joseph’s distinctive scene details.

5. Interpretation: divine providence, free will, promise, or protective release. 6.
Authority: who may speak for a family, enslaved woman, child, or promise? 7.
Human consequence: what happens when religious narratives are used to
marginalize descendants or to protect vulnerable people?

Prompt: “Write a claim about divine providence as an explicitly labeled theological

Part 7 — Closing (5 minutes)
“If the characters cannot command the fulfillment of a promise, what legitimate
authority does any later institution have to claim ownership of the promise or
everyone named within it?”

Independent study
Choose Genesis 22 with Qur’an 37:100–107, or Genesis 39–45 with Qur’an Surah 12.
Write a source-grounded comparison and a distinct paragraph giving a possible
interpretation. Do not merge those paragraphs.

Facilitator restraint
Do not require agreement that the specific events occurred; do not treat Muslim or
Jewish interpretation as a footnote to Christianity; and do not skip Hagar’s unequal
power.

UNIT 4 | Moses, Liberation, the Sea, and the Wilderness
Oppression, commission, revelation, collective responsibility, and delay

Duration: 75 minutes • Sources are witnesses to be examined, not mandatory beliefs.


Purpose
Moses is a sustained narrative connection across Torah, Christian biblical
collections, and Qur’an. Participants examine who exercises force, who resists it,
how public claims of revelation are tested, and whether liberation resolves or
simply changes power relationships.

Primary reading set
Exodus 1–3, 5–6, 12–17, 19–20, 32; Numbers 11, 13–14; Deuteronomy 1; Qur’an 20:9–
97, 28:3–35, 26:52–68, 7:142–160, 5:20–26; Jubilees 47–50 (selected).

Learning outcomes
     Trace a shared oppression–commission–departure–sea sequence.
     Distinguish what archaeology confirms about ancient Israel from what it does
      not confirm about an Exodus ruler.
     Compare Aaron’s role in Exodus 32 with al-Samiri and Aaron in Qur’an 20.
     Analyze competing meanings of instruction, obedience, communal consent, and
      wilderness delay.

Part 1 — Power before interpretation (7 minutes)
Ask: “What are observable signs of coerced labor or an authoritarian ruler? What is
the difference between claiming liberation and producing it?” List wages, mobility,
threats, obligations, and practical ability to refuse.

Connect to the earlier Unit 1 question: who controls another person’s time?

Part 2 — Exodus and Qur’anic Moses (18 minutes)
Read Exodus 1–3 and 14 with Qur’an 28:3–35 and 26:52–68. Identify threatened
birth, rescue, flight, encounter at fire, confrontations, departure, and sea
deliverance.

Before drawing moral conclusions, ask students to record precise agents: who
speaks, who commands, who obeys, who pursues, who is endangered? Which
details are absent from one account?

Note that the precise historical dating and scale of the Exodus remain debated. The
presence of Egypt and Pharaohs is independently established, but that does not
identify the ruler of the biblical story.

Part 3 — Tablets, golden calf, water, food (15 minutes)
Compare Exodus 32 with Qur’an 20:83–97. Genesis and Qur’an are not
interchangeable: in Exodus Aaron fashions the calf after the people demand a god;
in Qur’an 20 al-Samiri is charged with leading the making/worship, and Aaron
reports warning the people.

APPENDIX C | STILLPOINT AS A TEST CASE
This is an optional case study about the difference between mathematical structure,
religious conviction, and binding civic rules. It is deliberately not an independent
historical or biblical command.

Definition before arithmetic
StillPoint is a proposed, recurring protected interval in which ordinary institutional
demands upon human time are limited. The founder understands the Sabbath
through Jesus Christ. A civic implementation would provide protections without
requiring the beneficiary to confess that theology.

Three distinguishable claims
8. Mathematical statement: the seven-day week has 168 standard hours; one-
   seventh is 24 hours; one-fifth is 33 hours and 36 minutes.
9. Theological statement: a 24-hour Sabbath nested within the larger protected
   interval may be interpreted as an expression of Jesus Christ’s concern for
   human beings (compare Mark 2:27). This is not an explicit scriptural mandate
   for one-fifth.
10. Civic proposal: establish one-fifth as a minimum release from ordinary
   institutional demands, with precise rules for care, emergency work,
   employment, equity, compensation, and dissent.

Critical limits
A 364-day schematic year is exactly 52 seven-day weeks. This arithmetical identity
does not establish astronomical synchrony. The calendar’s civic purpose and
practical coordination rules can be debated separately from seasonal alignment. A
local sunset-based start and sunrise-based end vary in duration; they are not
automatically an exact 33h36m interval.

The protection covers everybody on equal terms without pretending that a fifth of a
week automatically eliminates inequality. Civilizations would need enforceable
safeguards so that privileged groups do not rest by transferring all necessary work
to vulnerable people.
